01 — Overview

Access wasn't the problem. Returning was.

Most language products already provide lessons, vocabulary exercises, progress tracking and speaking tools. My research suggested that access to learning content was not the main problem. Learners were starting, but finding it difficult to continue when work became busy, progress felt slow or the next step was unclear.

I led the product design from research and synthesis through to information architecture, interaction design and a high-fidelity clickable prototype.

The brief came down to one learner: someone who opens the app after a busy day, has ten minutes available and should not need to plan an entire study session before they can begin.

The product did not need to offer more ways to learn. It needed to make returning feel easier.
02 — What Changed the Brief

The challenge was consistency, not access

I began by comparing Duolingo, Babbel and Rosetta Stone, then surveyed 10 language learners and conducted three deeper interviews.

The existing products already offered strong learning foundations. Duolingo used gamification and competition. Babbel focused on practical conversation. Rosetta Stone leaned into immersion and pronunciation. The opportunity was not another variation of the same lesson library.

Survey findings. Research showed that learners were struggling to fit practice into their routines, maintain speaking confidence and understand whether their effort was leading somewhere meaningful.

  • Every participant rated consistency as at least moderately difficult No respondent described maintaining a routine as easy — this was a shared, not isolated, problem.

Three deeper interviews. Learners wanted flexible practice, more confidence using the language in real situations, and progress that felt meaningful rather than decorative.

This changed the design question from "How might we help people learn a language?" to "How might we make language practice easier to begin, useful to complete and clear enough to return to?"
03 — Key Decisions

Six decisions that shaped Gauge

Each decision traded a feature-first instinct for one that reduced the effort of returning.

1Continue learning before exploring everything else

Language apps often place lessons, games, challenges, live classes and promotions together on the home screen. That gives learners choice, but it also creates another decision before practice can begin. For someone already struggling with consistency, the product was asking them to plan their learning every time they returned.

I made Continue learning the primary action on the Gauge home screen. It surfaced the next recommended activity based on the learner's current path, while live classes, Gauge Pal, podcasts and other practice formats remained available as secondary options.

The intention was not to remove choice. It was to make the most useful choice easier.

2Onboarding had to earn every question

Language selection, learning goals and proficiency assessments can improve personalisation, but they also delay the first useful experience. The early onboarding flow asked learners to make several decisions before reaching a lesson. Testing showed this made the product feel more demanding than the short, flexible learning experience it promised.

I reduced the flow to the information needed to suggest an initial learning path:

Language selectionCurrent proficiencyPrimary learning goalAvailable practice time

Secondary preferences could be collected later, once the learner had experienced the product's value. The result was a shorter route from opening the app to beginning the first activity.

3Progress needed to show direction, not just completion

Most progress screens explain what has already happened — lessons completed, points earned, streak length and time spent. Research showed that visibility alone was not enough. Learners could see that they had been active, but were not always sure what they had improved or what they should do next.

I structured progress around three questions:

What did I complete?

Lessons, practice activities and live sessions.

What improved?

Vocabulary, listening, speaking or another skill area.

What should I do next?

A recommended lesson, practice activity or class.

This changed progress from a passive record into part of the learning journey.

4Practice had to work around time and confidence

Learners did not always have the same amount of time, energy or confidence available. Some wanted structured lessons. Others wanted to practise quietly during a commute, write without the pressure of speaking, or prepare before joining a live conversation.

Bite-sized lessons

Structured learning for short, focused sessions.

Gauge Pal

Low-pressure conversational practice with an AI-supported partner.

Curated podcasts

Listening practice matched to interests and proficiency.

Live classes

Real conversation with tutors and native speakers.

The product could recommend the most appropriate activity without forcing every learner through the same format.

5Rewards needed to unlock something worth earning

Points and streaks can create short-term activity, but they risk becoming disconnected from why someone wanted to learn in the first place.

Gauge connected rewards to live practice. Completing lessons and practice activities earned points that could be used toward live classes. Consistent self-guided learning therefore created access to an experience learners valued: real conversation.

The reward was not simply another badge. It moved learners closer to the confidence-building activity they found difficult to access.

6Feedback had to close the loop

Completing a lesson should not feel like reaching a dead end. The results experience showed what the learner completed, where they performed well, which areas needed more practice, how the activity affected their progress, and the most useful next action.

This created a repeatable learning loop: Continue → practise → receive feedback → understand progress → choose the next step.

The interface carried more of the responsibility for maintaining momentum.

04 — The Learning Loop

One connected journey instead of six separate features

The initial concept included lessons, flashcards, podcasts, AI practice, live classes, progress tracking and rewards. The design challenge was not creating each feature individually — it was making them feel like parts of the same product.

The end-to-end journey
1
Choose a language and complete the proficiency check
2
Receive a personalised starting point
3
Continue with a recommended lesson
4
Complete a practice activity
5
Understand the result and skill progress
6
Earn progress toward a meaningful reward
7
Return to a clear next action

This flow became the structure behind the prototype. Each screen had to support one of three things: help the learner begin, help the learner complete the current activity, or help the learner understand what comes next.

05 — Validation

The first prototype made learning available, but not always obvious

I tested three core journeys: beginning to learn, completing an activity, and understanding progress. The first round exposed three major areas of friction.

  • Onboarding felt longer than expected Participants understood the questions but wanted to reach the first activity sooner.
  • The home screen offered too many equal choices Several participants explored secondary features before recognising the main continuation path.
  • Progress showed activity without enough direction Learners could see what they had completed, but were less certain about what to do next.

These findings shaped the next iteration: shortened onboarding, a dominant Continue learning action, clearer screen hierarchy, more explicit lesson-completion feedback, progress linked directly to the next activity, and simplified reward explanations.

The strongest improvement came from reducing decision-making at the moments when learners were most likely to stop. Participants no longer needed to interpret the full product before taking action. 06 — Outcome

A validated learning journey built around returning

Gauge developed from an open-ended language-learning concept into a validated, clickable mobile product centred on one repeatable behaviour:

Open the app → continue learning → complete an activity → understand progress → know what comes next.

Two rounds of usability testing increased unassisted task completion from 63% to 88% and reduced critical usability issues from eight to three. Most importantly, seven of eight participants understood their next learning step without guidance.

The project did not prove long-term retention — that would require a live product tested over several weeks. What it did prove was that the redesigned experience made the core journey easier to complete and the next action easier to understand.
The largest shift was not a new feature. It was changing the product from a collection of learning tools into a system that continuously guides the learner forward.
What I'd Measure Next

Testing whether clarity leads to consistency

A live beta would test whether improved clarity translates into sustained behaviour:

  • Onboarding completion
  • First-lesson completion
  • Day-one and day-seven retention
  • Weekly learning frequency
  • Return rate after viewing progress
  • Use of Gauge Pal and live classes
  • Reward redemption
  • Learner confidence over time

The next product question is no longer whether learners understand what to do. It is whether that clarity is strong enough to help them keep doing it.

The redesign didn't add another way to learn. It made the one that mattered — coming back tomorrow — easier to repeat.
