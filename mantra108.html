01 — Where We Started

The page had the information, but not the feeling

Kirtan is a group chanting and meditation practice, usually set to live music. Mantra108 runs weekly Kirtan sessions in Melbourne, alongside other community events built around music, meditation and connection.

The original page had grown section by section. Each part had value on its own, but together they didn't add up to a coherent journey:

  • Wellbeing stats, meditation explanations, Kirtan history, photography, a testimonial and booking info — all bolted on over time
  • Visitors hit a booking action before understanding what Kirtan was
  • Benefits were buried inside paragraphs instead of standing out
  • History appeared before the modern experience had been established
  • Photography documented the event but rarely showed what attending would feel like

The page could describe Kirtan.

It could not help someone imagine being there.
02 — Evidence

Evidence that the experience was not working

I didn't want to redesign based on visual judgement alone, so I combined analytics, behavioural data and lightweight user testing.

Analytics review. Google Analytics showed a 68% bounce rate among first-time visitors — a sign the page wasn't giving people enough reason to stay.

First-time visitor bounce rate68%

Heatmap and scroll-depth analysis. Data showed roughly 40% drop-off between the "About Kirtan" content and the booking CTA. Attention weakened at dense sections, and reassurance appeared too late. The issue wasn't page length — it was what visitors met along the way.

Drop-off before the booking CTA40%

Heatmap overlay, scroll to see the full page — used to locate friction points

Five unmoderated user tests. I asked five participants unfamiliar with Mantra108 to explain what Kirtan was, what a session involved, and whether they felt comfortable attending. Three of five couldn't confidently explain Kirtan afterwards, and several hesitated to book because they didn't know what would be expected of them.

Visitors did not need more information. They needed the existing information explained and sequenced differently.
03 — Diagnosis

Diagnosing the real problem

The content audit, heatmaps and user tests pointed to three connected issues.

  • No plain-language explanation The headline, "Kirtan Gives Meditation Wings," worked for existing participants but stayed abstract for first-timers.
  • No clear reassurance for beginners Visitors weren't told whether they needed musical, meditation or spiritual experience — they had to infer beginners were welcome, and testing showed this uncertainty affected booking.
  • The wrong information sequence The booking action appeared before enough explanation or reassurance — the page asked for commitment before it earned confidence.
This reframed the project from "How might we modernise the Kirtan page?" to "How might we help someone unfamiliar with Kirtan understand the experience, feel comfortable participating and confidently register?"
04 — Approach

Mapping the visitor's questions

Five conversations weren't enough to responsibly build demographic personas — that would mean generalising from a handful of people to an entire audience. But they were enough to spot the same questions recurring. I structured the experience around those questions instead of assumptions about who visitors were:

Why should I care about this?What is Kirtan?Is this suitable for someone like me?Do I need any experience?What happens during a session?Who else attends?Who is behind the event?When and where does it happen?How do I join?

These questions became the new information architecture — the page followed the order confidence needed to develop, not the order content had originally been added.

05 — The Redesign

Designing the new journey

Nine decisions carried the page from a collection of sections into a single guided journey.

1Lead with an emotional outcome

The original headline — "Kirtan Gives Meditation Wings" — required visitors to grasp two unfamiliar ideas before finding any meaning.

The new headline, "Feel Lighter," led with an accessible emotional outcome — backed by energetic photography, a concise explanation and two pathways: Try a Kirtan Session and See Why Others Love It. Its job wasn't to explain everything, just to give people a reason to continue.

After

Visitors progressing beyond the hero61% → 79%

An 18-point improvement — the clearer promise, stronger imagery and more direct copy gave visitors more reason to explore.

2Make the benefits understandable within seconds

The original page buried Kirtan's value inside wellbeing statistics and long paragraphs — useful, but visitors had to dig for the meaning.

I converted them into a scannable card system: Release Stress, Zero Pressure, Feel Something, Meet Real People, Pocket Friendly, Soul Party. The language stayed conversational — Mantra108 wasn't a clinical meditation programme, and the interface needed the same energy as the event.

3Explain the unfamiliar, directly

A heading like "About Kirtan" described the content but ignored the visitor's uncertainty. I replaced it with "Wait… What Even Is Kirtan?" — the question first-timers were already asking.

The explanation was shortened and paired with real photography, describing what people would hear, see and do rather than leaning on spiritual terminology. This set four content principles for the wider system:

Direct

Use language people understand immediately.

Conversational

Reflect questions visitors may already be thinking.

Reassuring

Address uncertainty without treating it as an objection.

Human

Describe the experience through feelings, participation and real people.

4Remove uncertainty before asking for commitment

The user tests surfaced a recurring concern — "What will be expected of me?" The original page never said whether visitors had to sing, meditate or already understand the practice.

I introduced "Why Kirtan Hits Different" directly before booking — No Experience Needed, No Pressure to Do It Right, Big Energy, Soft Heart. The page stopped assuming people would infer they were welcome. It told them.

Behavioural impact

After reassurance content was introduced before the booking action, exits in the pre-CTA area decreased by 12 percentage points.

Drop-off at the broader friction point40% → 24%

A 16-point improvement, or a 40% relative reduction — the clearest signal that sequencing reassurance before registration helped more visitors continue.

5Show the experience instead of explaining it again

Kirtan depends on sound, rhythm, movement and shared participation — no paragraph could communicate that atmosphere. I introduced a dedicated "What to Expect in a Session" video so visitors could see the musicians, the audience, the environment and the pace of the event.

As the experienced visual designer, I storyboarded the visual journey before filming, so the video team had a clear shot list instead of capturing footage and hoping it told the right story.

View Video
When an experience is difficult to explain, show it.

Visitors could begin forming their own impression instead of depending entirely on Mantra108's description.

6Replace brand claims with human proof

The original page had one testimonial — enough to support the experience, not enough to show the range of people attending or why they valued it.

I introduced a reusable testimonial system with multiple attendee voices, followed by "Who's Behind This" — portraits, names, roles and short statements introducing the team. Trust came from people, not just brand language.

7Turn attendance into belonging

Not every visitor is ready to register. Some connect with Mantra108's purpose and want to contribute another way. "Want to Join the Crew?" introduced volunteering as a separate pathway:

Booking answers

"How can I attend?"

Volunteering answers

"How can I become part of this?"

What didn't work as expected

The volunteering CTA landed just a 1.2% click-through rate — well below the primary registration pathway. The proposition, timing or placement likely isn't strong enough yet, and visitors may need a clearer sense of what's involved.

Volunteer CTA click-through rate1.2%

Rather than treating every addition as a success, I've kept volunteering as an unresolved hypothesis for further testing.

8Preserve the history, but change its position

Kirtan's history deserved to stay on the page — the issue was never the content, only where visitors encountered it. Previously, history appeared before visitors understood the contemporary event.

I moved it lower and reframed it as a concise origin story. By then, visitors already understood the offer, what Kirtan was, whether beginners were welcome, and who ran it — so history could deepen understanding instead of interrupting it.

9Make registration the conclusion

The original page asked visitors to book before answering their most important questions. Registration stayed accessible throughout the redesign, but the strongest push appeared only after visitors had an emotional reason to continue, a plain-language explanation, reassurance, visual evidence, attendee voices and team info.

The final "When & Where" section grouped schedule, location, imagery and registration — practical info became the journey's natural conclusion, not a disconnected ask.

06 — Design System

From one page to a scalable design system

This wasn't meant to be another one-off landing page. Mantra108 regularly launches new events — a fresh interface each time would just reproduce the original inconsistency. So I translated the redesign into a system across four layers.

Foundations

Colour, typography, spacing, imagery and interaction roles.

Components

Reusable interface modules with defined responsibilities.

Content Patterns

Consistent ways to explain, reassure and prompt action.

Page Architecture

A repeatable sequence from curiosity to registration.

The goal wasn't identical pages — it was pages that are easier to create, maintain, and still feel like Mantra108.

Colour was given a responsibility. Purple was already core to the brand, but its use wasn't consistent — buttons, backgrounds and decoration all competed for attention. So I mapped each colour to one job:

#491976 — Primary
Headlines and key actions. It carries the brand.
#e369a2 — Accent
Highlights and energy. Used sparingly.
#f6e2ff — Surface
Soft backgrounds. Gives content room to breathe.

Three colours, three jobs. That's enough for a small brand to stay consistent without a huge palette to manage.

Typography split into two clear jobs. One font to grab attention, one font to be read.

Feel Lighter
Headline — Antique Olive Std

Kirtan is your soul's Spotify Mantras, an immersive sound journey, and group energy.

Body — Instrument Sans

Antique Olive Std is bold and unusual — good for headlines people notice while scrolling.

Instrument Sans is quiet and clean. It's built for reading, so longer paragraphs don't feel tiring.

One expressive font, one readable font. That pairing does most of the work.

Photography was given a job too. The originals were authentic, but scale, placement and purpose varied. In the new system, every image type had a defined role:

Hero

Create emotional relevance.

Experience

Demonstrate participation and atmosphere.

Community

Communicate connection and belonging.

Profile

Introduce the people behind the event.

Context

Support cultural and historical information.

Every module had one job. The page became a family of reusable modules, not independent sections. Content can change; the decision logic stays consistent — that's what makes it scalable rather than a fixed template.

07 — Responsive

Preserving the journey across every screen

Desktop used space, imagery and multi-column layouts to create energy. Mobile couldn't lean on that composition — it had to preserve the journey through sequence, spacing and interaction alone.

  • Multi-column cards moved into a logical reading order
  • Primary actions expanded for easier interaction
  • Text and imagery rearranged without changing the narrative
  • Image cropping remained consistent
  • Decorative elements were reduced when they competed with content
  • Testimonials and benefit groups became swipeable or stacked
  • Event details remained grouped
  • The visitor journey stayed unchanged
The layout changed across screen sizes. The decision-making sequence did not.

Attendance rose 20% during the rollout. The redesign likely contributed — but the rollout also coincided with an Instagram campaign and a better time slot. So attendance is presented as a contributed outcome, not something design caused alone.

A reusable foundation. The project also delivered:

10 — What I'd Do Differently

A tighter measurement plan next time

The before-and-after data showed meaningful improvement, but a more controlled measurement plan would strengthen the findings. If repeating this, I would:

  • Define success metrics before beginning the redesign
  • Track booking starts and completed registrations separately
  • Measure mobile and desktop conversion independently
  • Run a controlled full-funnel A/B test
  • Avoid changing marketing, scheduling and page design during the same measurement window
  • Test the volunteering proposition as a separate journey
  • Conduct moderated follow-up sessions after launch

That would make it easier to isolate individual decisions and see where visitors still needed support.

Reflection

Order is the design decision

The most important design decision was not the new headline, colour palette or component library.

It was changing the order in which the story was told.

The original page followed the structure of an organisation explaining itself. The redesigned page followed the mental journey of someone deciding whether to join — that shift shaped everything after it.

The system does not make every event page look identical. It makes every event feel recognisably Mantra108.
